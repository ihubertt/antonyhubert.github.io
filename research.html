Production of soft magnetic FeSi6.5 parts with precise layer topography using integrated laser remelting at PBF-LB/M

What the paper reports, in plain terms

Electric motors lose energy to eddy currents. The standard fix is lamination: stack thin, individually insulated steel sheets so the current has no continuous path to flow along.

FeSi6.5 is the best alloy for that job — up to 30% lower core losses than conventional silicon steel. It is also so brittle that it shatters in a rolling mill, so it cannot be made into sheet, so almost nobody uses it. The industry designs motors around what the mill can produce rather than what the magnetic circuit wants.

3D printing never asks the material to survive being bent or stamped, so it removes that barrier — but it introduces a new one. Printed surfaces are rough, and a rough layer needs a thicker insulating coat, which makes the laminations thicker, which brings the eddy-current losses straight back.

What this paper shows: remelting the top layer with a widened laser beam, in the gentle heat-conduction regime, makes it 38% smoother in a single pass. Repeating the pass during the build brings the surface down to Sa 1.77 µm, the precision needed for thin insulating layers. Remelting does not change how the brittle alloy cracks, and it only slightly modifies its grain structure.

40–50% of total energy losses in these machines are eddy-current lossesThe single largest addressable loss mechanism
FeSi6.5 cuts core losses by up to 30% vs. conventional silicon steelAnd it cannot be rolled, stamped or stacked — which is why nobody uses it

The best available soft magnetic alloy is locked out of production by its own brittleness. Printing is the only route that never asks the material to survive deformation.

The four findings

1 — The surface gets dramatically smoother

A single remelting pass with a 300 µm beam at 325 W and 800 mm/s cut the top-layer roughness from Sa 4.55 µm to 2.81 µm (38.3%). Built into the process cyclically, the same exposure reached Sa 1.77 µm with every layer, more than 41% below the reference parts in the same build, and Sa 1.81 µm with every fifth layer. In the thesis, tactile Ra fell below 1.0 µm with every-layer remelting.

2 — A wider beam changes the melt regime

The build-up runs at about 59 kW/mm² in the deep-penetration regime, with melt tracks about 259 µm deep, more than six layer heights. Defocusing to a 300 µm beam cut the average intensity to 4.5 kW/mm². Up to 350 W the remelt then stays in a stable heat-conduction regime, only 52-82 µm deep, and the scan tracks flow together into a closed, homogeneous surface. From 375 W heat builds up and the process flips back towards deep penetration.

Every-layer remelting slightly modifies the grain structure, with sporadic epitaxial grain growth across several tracks and layers.

3 — Remelting does not change how the alloy cracks

FeSi6.5 shows sporadic cracks in the layer surface, and remelting neither adds to them nor removes them; they are what limits the achievable surface quality. Remelting every layer also rounds the part edges and builds up material at the contour. Remelting every fifth layer gives almost the same surface with less loss of accuracy, which makes it the practical setting.

4 — Two effects that look like one, separated

Molten metal building up at the part edges scales with power intensity. How deep the remelt reaches scales with areal energy intensity. Treating them as two separate levers is what made a stable processing window achievable — a single volumetric energy density figure blends them together and cannot resolve the trade-off.

That is the difference between tuning a process and understanding it.
